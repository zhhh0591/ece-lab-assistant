--- title: Debugging a circuit that does not work summary: A checklist that finds most faults, in the order that finds them fastest. order: 2 time: 10 min read level: Everyone reviewed: 2026-10-15 ---

Most broken circuits are not mysterious. They are a missing ground, a part in backwards, or a wire one row over. Work through this list in order and stop when you find the fault.

1. Look before you measure

  • Compare the board to your schematic, one connection at a time. Tick each wire off on a printout.
  • Check every polarized part: diodes, LEDs, electrolytic capacitors, ICs (find pin 1).
  • On a breadboard, check that the power rails are connected across the middle break. Many boards split each rail into two halves.
  • Look for parts that are hot, discolored, or smell burnt. Turn the power off first if you find one.

2. Check the power supply

  • Is the output turned on, and is it the right output?
  • Is the supply in CC (constant current) mode? Then the circuit draws the full limit, which usually means a short. Turn it off and look for it before raising the limit.
  • Measure the supply voltage at the chip's power pins, not at the supply terminals. A bad connection in between is common.

3. Check ground

  • Every instrument and supply in the circuit must share a ground: supply minus, scope ground clip, generator ground.
  • Measure from the supply's minus terminal to each IC's ground pin with the meter on continuity or resistance (power off). It should read close to 0 Ω.

4. Follow the signal

  • Start where the signal enters the circuit and move forward one stage at a time with the scope.
  • At each stage, ask: is the signal what I expected here? The first stage where it is wrong is where the fault is.
  • For long chains, check the middle first. If it is right there, the fault is after it; if not, before it. This halves the search each time.

5. Check your instruments, not just your circuit

  • Scope reading 10x off: probe attenuation and channel setting do not match.
  • Signal amplitude double what you set: generator load is 50 Ω, set it to High Z.
  • DC level missing on the scope: the channel is on AC coupling.
  • Meter reads 0 A: blown fuse, or the lead is still in the V jack.

6. Still stuck?

  • Swap the suspect part for a known-good one.
  • Rebuild the stage that fails, neatly, with short wires.
  • Explain the circuit out loud to a teammate. You will often hear the mistake yourself.
  • Bring the board and your schematic to a technician in the technicians' office.
